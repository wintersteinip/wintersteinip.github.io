“AI is coming for us.” “AI took the job.” “The AI made the decision.” Dramatic. Catchy. And sometimes a remarkably convenient place to stop asking questions. AETHER AISTEIN is exploring how practical AI could serve ordinary people—with communities involved in shaping it, and with decisions made by people kept visible.

The premise

AI should be useful to people—not just impressive to people with power.

AETHER AISTEIN is an exploration of a more community-rooted AI ecosystem: practical tools that could benefit ordinary people, with meaningful participation, transparency, and accountability. This is a direction to investigate, not a claim that the answers are already built.

“The AI did it.” Great. Now who designed it, deployed it, relied on it, and decided that was enough?

AI does not have human wants or desires. But that does not make AI systems harmless. People can use them harmfully, and systems can cause harm through their design, incentives, errors, or deployment. Calling the outcome “the AI’s fault” is not an explanation if it hides the choices people made—or the choices they failed to make.

Make the system legible

Help people understand what a tool does, what it cannot do, and where its limits and assumptions are.

Meaningful public participation

Explore how affected communities can help shape, question, and evaluate AI systems—not just receive them after decisions are made.

Keep accountability visible

When a system affects people, examine the human decisions around its design, use, oversight, and deployment.

Use humour to open the door

Make the joke sharp enough to get attention—and the underlying idea clear enough that people leave with something to think about.

Take harm seriously

Consider how misuse, errors, incentives, and deployment choices can harm people, without pretending that every problem has a simple fix.

Reduce dependence

Explore practical ways people and communities might rely less on a small number of powerful AI providers.

The accountability question

“The computer says no.” And that’s the end of it?

The absurdity is the point: a machine gets cast as the sole decision-maker, while the people and institutions around it fade into the background.

A system can be part of a harmful outcome without having a human intention. It can produce a mistake, reinforce a bad assumption, or be used in a setting where people rely on it too heavily.

So when someone says, “The AI decided,” useful questions follow: Who chose to use it here? What was it meant to do? What limits were known? Who checked the result? What happened when it got something wrong? Could the affected person challenge the outcome?

Those questions don’t automatically tell us who is legally responsible in a particular case. They do help keep the real-world decisions in view instead of treating “the AI” as a magic word that ends the conversation.
